# Lunar Volatile Depletion

The Moon is systematically depleted in volatile and moderately volatile elements (S, Cl, K, Zn, Rb, Pb) relative to Earth and Mars. Stable isotope fractionation patterns in lunar mare basalts provide evidence for a global-scale evaporation event during lunar formation or differentiation.

## Key Observations

| Element | Lunar signature | Earth/Mars comparison |
|---------|----------------|----------------------|
| Zn | δ⁶⁶Zn ≈ +1.4‰; abundance 2 ppm | Earth: δ⁶⁶Zn = +0.28‰; 37 ppm |
| Cl | δ³⁷Cl 0 to +24‰ in different phases | Earth/Mars: −1 to +0.5‰ |
| K | Slight heavy-isotope enrichment in mare basalts | Earth K/U ≈ 11,800; Moon K/U ≈ 3,000 |
| S | δ³⁴S = +0.57‰ in mare basalts | Earth MORB: −0.98‰ |

## Two-Phase Volatile Depletion Model

1. **Nebular depletion**: Pre-planetary, hot dusty solar nebula removes volatile elements uniformly across rocky bodies
2. **Planetary depletion**: Secondary event specific to each body, correlated with escape velocity — smaller, airless bodies lose more volatiles

Moon escape velocity (2.38 km/s) vs. Earth (11.19 km/s) explains the disparity in volatile retention.

## Candidate Mechanisms

| Mechanism | Evidence | Problems |
|-----------|----------|---------|
| Giant impact evaporation | Explains Moon-Earth co-genesis; high-energy event | Requires preferential volatile retention by Earth |
| Stochastic late accretion | Explains higher volatile content of Earth post-Moon formation | Requires ~12% late accretion mass for Zn; inconsistent with HSE budgets |
| Lunar magma ocean outgassing | Non-ideal Rayleigh fractionation matches observed δ⁶⁶Zn; explains early cumulate heterogeneity | Best current model |

## Non-Ideal Rayleigh Fractionation

Mare basalt Zn shows δ⁶⁶Zn ≈ +1.3‰, far below the ideal Rayleigh distillation prediction (>5‰) for their low Zn abundances. This indicates inefficient evaporation, consistent with diffusion-limited outgassing from a lunar magma ocean rather than catastrophic evaporation.

## Isotopic Fractionation Principle

Heavy isotopes preferentially remain in the condensed phase during evaporation; lighter isotopes are enriched in the vapor phase. The Moon's heavy isotopic signature in volatile elements reflects residual melt after extensive volatile loss.
