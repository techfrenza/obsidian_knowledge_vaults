# Lunar Volatile Depletion via Non-Ideal Rayleigh Fractionation

## Technical Core Logic

Ideal Rayleigh fractionation: if evaporation is complete and fast (equilibrium), isotope fractionation is maximal. For zinc at lunar Zn abundances (~2 ppm vs Earth's 37 ppm), ideal Rayleigh predicts δ⁶⁶Zn >5‰.

Observed: δ⁶⁶Zn ≈ +1.4‰ in lunar mare basalts.

This 4× gap is diagnostic: it means evaporation was **not** complete or instantaneous. The non-ideal result is explained by:
- Diffusion-limited transport from a liquid magma ocean
- Isotopically lighter Zn evaporates faster, but slow diffusion to the surface creates a depleted surface layer that limits further evaporation
- Net result: less fractionation than ideal equilibrium, leaving the distinctive +1.4‰ signature

## Trade-off of Formation Models

| Model | Prediction | Observed | Match? |
|-------|-----------|---------|--------|
| Giant impact flash evaporation | δ⁶⁶Zn >5‰ | +1.4‰ | No |
| Lunar magma ocean diffusion-limited outgassing | Non-ideal low δ⁶⁶Zn; cumulate heterogeneity | +1.4‰ + heterogeneity | Yes |
| Stochastic late accretion | High volatile content post-formation | Low volatile content | No |
| Nebular depletion only | Similar Zn to Earth | 20x lower Zn abundance | Partial |

## Broader Implication

The same stable isotope mass spectrometry used to detect extraterrestrial amino acid indigenicity (δ¹³C, δD in [[Stable Isotopes as Biomarkers]]) also constrains planetary formation timescales. The Moon's isotope "fingerprint" is not just about volatiles — it constrains the entire timeline of planetary formation and determines which bodies in the solar system could have retained water for habitability.
