## Technical Core Logic

JWST uses transmission spectroscopy: it reads starlight filtered through a planet's atmosphere during a transit. This technique amplifies signal for large, puffy, H₂-rich atmospheres (sub-Neptunes like K2-18b). For thin Earth-like atmospheres around Sun-like stars, it fails entirely — the planet-to-star brightness contrast is too low to separate signals.

ELT uses direct imaging with adaptive optics (deformable mirrors that counteract atmospheric turbulence in real time). It can:
- Build up signal over months without needing a transit
- Detect thinner atmospheres JWST cannot see
- Eventually observe planets around Sun-like stars with HWO (2040s)

JWST has already revealed that most M-dwarf rocky planets (TRAPPIST-1b) may have no atmosphere at all — stripped by stellar activity. This makes ELT the primary life-detection avenue for the 2030s.

## Trade-off Table

| Capability | JWST (space) | ELT (ground) |
|-----------|-------------|--------------|
| Target planet type | Sub-Neptunes, H₂-rich atmospheres | Rocky planets, thin atmospheres |
| Method | Transmission spectroscopy (needs transit) | Direct imaging (no transit needed) |
| Limitation | Cannot study Sun-like star rocky planets | Ground atmospheric interference |
| 2030s relevance | Diminishing for rocky planets | Primary rocky planet instrument |

## Depth Insight

The counterintuitive result: for finding life on Earth-analog planets, the $10 billion space telescope is the wrong architecture. A ground-based 39-meter mirror with adaptive optics — still subject to Earth's atmosphere — can do what JWST cannot. The limitation is not space vs. ground; it is transit geometry vs. direct imaging.
