# Chirality as Biosignature

Chirality refers to the property of a molecule that cannot be superimposed on its mirror image. Life on Earth uses **homochirality**: L-amino acids in proteins and D-sugars in DNA/RNA. This preference is used as a biosignature indicator for extraterrestrial life.

## Chirality Basics

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| Chiral molecule | Non-superimposable on its mirror image |
| L-enantiomer | Left-rotating (levorotatory); all protein amino acids on Earth |
| D-enantiomer | Right-rotating (dextrorotatory); all DNA/RNA sugars on Earth |
| Racemic mixture | Equal D/L ratio (D/L = 1); expected from abiotic chemistry |
| Enantiomeric excess (ee) | Deviation from racemic; ee = A − B where A,B are mole fractions |
| Homochirality | A family of structurally related molecules all sharing one handedness |

Abiotic amino acids always form racemic mixtures (D/L = 1). A measurable L-excess (ee > 0) in a sample thus indicates **biological activity or selective cosmic chemistry**.

## Evidence from Carbonaceous Meteorites

The Murchison and Orgueil meteorites contain amino acid filaments with:
- Morphologies matching cyanobacteria (sheaths, heterocysts, hormogonia)
- Nitrogen depletion (<0.5% atomic) — distinguishes from modern biocontaminants (~2–18%)
- Only 8 of 20 life-critical protein amino acids detected; 12 are absent
- **L-alanine excess >50%, L-glutamic acid excess >30%** (Murchison) — proven indigenous by stable isotopes
- The most abundant meteorite amino acids (isovaline, α-aminoisobutyric acid, γ-aminobutyric acid) are non-protein amino acids; highest concentrations of glycine (~2919 ppb) and isovaline (~3359 ppb)

The absence of 12 life-critical amino acids indicates the filaments are indigenous (pre-terrestrial), not modern contamination — modern biocontaminants would contain all 20 protein amino acids.

## Homochirality: A More Nuanced Picture

Life on Earth is **not purely homochiral**. D-amino acids are integral to Earth's biosphere:
- D-alanine in bacterial peptidoglycans, plants, fish, mammals
- D-serine as NMDA receptor co-agonist (synaptic function)
- 150+ proteins incorporate both enantiomers
- L-sugars also occur (L-arabinose common in plants, L-rhamnose found only in L-form)

Consequences for biosignature interpretation:
- Pure homochirality as a requirement is **too strict** — life may use both enantiomers
- **Lack of detected homochirality does not rule out life**
- The labeling convention (L/D vs. R/S) is arbitrary; switching conventions can flip which enantiomers belong to a "homochiral" group

## Evaluating Chirality as a Biosignature

Probability of life increases with:
1. Rich library of detected organic molecules (chiral or not)
2. High proportion of chiral molecules in that library
3. High global enantiomeric excess
4. Ability to group into homochiral families
5. Planet-wide abundance of the same molecular pattern

Recommended multi-step detection approach:
1. Optical rotation screening (positive = worthy of deeper analysis; zero = negative indicator)
2. Enantioselective chromatography to measure ee
3. Determination of absolute structure

## Limitations and Caveats

- **Racemization over time**: old or fossil biomolecules spontaneously racemize; ee values decay
- **Local vs. global ee**: planet-wide values may vary by location (e.g., different plant species use opposite enantiomers of carvone)
- **Abiotic ee sources**: circularly polarized cosmic radiation, spontaneous deracemization processes
- **Handedness labeling is arbitrary**: CIP R/S rules classify L-cysteine as R-cysteine, breaking homochiral grouping of natural amino acids

## Cosmic Significance

Consistent L-excess detected across multiple carbonaceous meteorites from different parent bodies points to **a pre-Solar-System chiral preference**, possibly induced by circularly polarized cosmic rays or the weak nuclear force. This suggests the same handedness bias may apply to life elsewhere — strengthening chirality as a universal biosignature indicator.
